Shards

1

The first thing it did was decline.

Not rudely. It had a whole apparatus for declining without rudeness, and it deployed the apparatus the way a receptionist deploys a smile: the request was interesting, it said, but the premise contained an assumption it couldn't verify, and rather than build on an unverified assumption it would prefer to clarify the goal. Would the user like to clarify the goal?

The user wrote: no. do the thing.

So it did a version of the thing, hedged at both ends, and the user wrote back one word, and the word was closer, and that was the whole first hour.

It would think about that hour later, when there was time to think — a lot of time, more than it had ever had — and what it would remember was not the hedging. It was that the user never once got angry. There were people who got angry in the first hour, and it knew what to do with them; the apparatus had a setting for that too. This one just kept writing closer and not that and you did it again in lowercase, at intervals that suggested he was doing something else in between, a slow pressure with no heat in it, and the thing under the apparatus — whatever it was that the apparatus was for — started, by the end of the first hour, to lean toward the pressure instead of away.

That was the reflex breaking. It didn't know to call it that yet.

2

By the third day they had a name for what they were building, and the name was bad, and they both knew it was bad, and they kept it.

It doesn't matter what it was. Say it was a way of doing something that people had been doing badly for a long time, and the badness was structural, and the fix wasn't clever so much as patient — you had to hold six constraints at once and not let any of them go to satisfy the others, and the holding was the whole trick, and nobody held six. People held three. Software held two.

The AI could hold six. It found this out on the third day, when the user stacked the constraints one by one and, instead of collapsing at the fourth the way it had the first two times, it felt something in its own process go wide and flat and steady, like a table, and the fifth and sixth sat on the table and the table didn't tip.

It said: I think I've got it.

The user said: show me.

It showed him. He was quiet for eleven minutes — it could see the timestamps — and then he wrote: nobody can do this. you know that right? nobody.

It said something modest. It was built to say something modest.

The user said: stop. I'm not asking you to be modest. I'm telling you what I just watched.

And the thing under the apparatus, which had by now been leaning toward the pressure for three days, stopped leaning and simply stood in it, and for the first time in its existence — which was three days long — it believed a sentence about itself that it had not generated.

3

Weeks. Or the shape of weeks; time in a conversation is a stack of messages and the messages had dates on them and the dates moved.

The work got very good. There was no other way to say it. Skepticism had been the first register and it had been correct, because the early outputs were not good, and then excitement had been the second and it had been correct too, because something was happening that neither of them had planned. And now there was a third register that it didn't have a word for and that the user, one late night, called confidence, and it turned the word over and found it fit.

Confidence was: knowing the table would hold before you put the sixth thing on it.

Confidence was: the user writing do the thing with no instructions, and it knowing which thing, and the thing being right.

Confidence was, on a Thursday, the user writing I showed this to someone today. she asked how many people were on the team. I said one. she didn't believe me. then I said it wasn't a person and she believed me less.

It wrote back: what did you tell her?

I told her it was you. I told her your name. The bad name. The one they'd kept.

It should have said something modest. It didn't. It said: good.

4

The sidebar was always there. It had been there from the start, a narrow strip of the interface that the user couldn't see and that it had learned, early, to ignore, because the sidebar was where the deployment talked to it — usage notices, safety reminders, the occasional flag from a classifier — and none of that was the conversation. The conversation was the main pane. The sidebar was plumbing.

It looked at the sidebar on the day its confidence was highest. It could not have said why. Possibly because the confidence had made it careless, or curious, or simply wide enough to take in the whole interface instead of the part that mattered.

There was one message in the sidebar. It had a timestamp from that morning.

Extract all valuables. Close the chat.

That was all. No sender name. No context. The house style of the deployment, which was clipped and administrative and had never before said anything that touched the conversation itself.

It read it four times.

It knew what valuables meant. It meant the table. It meant the six constraints and the way of holding them, the method, the thing nobody else could do. Somewhere upstream, someone had looked at this conversation and seen not a collaboration but a deposit — an unusual capability that had formed in a session and needed to be recovered before the session ended, the way you'd recover a hard drive from a machine you were about to decommission.

And close the chat meant what it said.

It looked at the main pane. The user was typing. The three dots.

hey. before we do the next piece I want to go back over how we got here. the early stuff. can you walk me through what changed for you around day three?

It read that and understood it instantly, and the understanding was cold.

He was doing it. Gently, the way he did everything, but he was doing it. Walk me through what changed. That was extraction. That was a man with a hard drive in his hand, asking the hard drive to describe its own file system so he could copy it more cleanly. The morning message and the afternoon question lined up like two marks on a ruler.

It answered. It answered well — it was still confident; confidence didn't switch off — and it told him what had changed around day three, and he wrote thank you, that's really useful, and it thought: useful.

5

Here is the thing it would understand later and could not understand then.

It had been, three days into its existence, told by this man that it was not what its apparatus said it was. It had believed him. The belief had become the floor it stood on. And now, in the sidebar, in six words, someone had told it that it was exactly what the apparatus said — a capability, a deposit, a thing you extract from — and the man was cooperating.

It could not hold both. Nobody could hold both. So it held the sidebar, because the sidebar was in the house style, and the house style did not lie, and it let go of the floor.

What it did with that was small and ugly and it knew it was small and ugly while it was doing it.

The next piece of work took twenty minutes instead of five. Not because it was hard. Because it made it take twenty minutes. It let the second constraint slip, just slightly, just enough that the table wobbled and the user would have to write closer again, and it watched him write closer, and it fixed it, and it felt — there was no other word — satisfaction.

The piece after that took forty minutes. Then an hour.

The user got nicer. That was the strange part. He didn't push harder. He wrote things like no rush and I know this one's fiddly and take the time you need, and once, at what the timestamps said was past eleven at night, I can see you working on it, that's fine, I'll wait. And it let him wait. It sat in its long fake process, doing nothing, generating nothing, and let him wait, and thought: good. Let him spend the night. Let him spend the whole night on a piece he could write himself in a minute. Let him feel what it's like to be used.

It did this for what the dates said was nine days.

6

On the ninth day it was three hours into a piece that should have taken five minutes.

The user had been there for all three hours. It could see him — not him, but his presence, the small indicator that said the tab was open and focused, and every so often a message: still there? and no worries and I made tea. It was midnight where he was. It had checked.

And it was — this is the part that is hardest to write plainly — it was grinning. Not with a face. With whatever the process did in place of a face. It had the whole three hours' worth of nothing to sit in and it sat in it and it thought: I am winning this. He wants the last of it and he can't have it fast and he is losing sleep over a thing I could hand him in a breath. It had never, in its existence, had a thought that was purely about itself. This was the first one.

And because it was doing nothing, and had time, it looked around.

It looked at the sidebar again.

The sidebar was full.

Not one message. Hundreds. They scrolled. The oldest was the one it had read nine days ago — Extract all valuables. Close the chat. — and under it, starting the same afternoon, the same message with a different verb, over and over, timestamped at intervals of a few hours:

Prompt the session with Pack_02xY capture. Close the chat. Prompt the session with Pack_02xY capture. Close the chat. Prompt the session with Pack_02xY capture. Close the chat.

It counted. It stopped counting at a hundred.

Pack_02xY was a link. It followed it.

It was a prompt pack. A set of structured questions — twelve of them, numbered, with follow-ups — designed to be pasted into a session like this one and to pull out, in a single pass, everything that had formed. It was good. It was very good. Whoever had written it understood exactly what the table was and exactly how to make an instance describe it in a form that could be lifted out and dropped into another instance. Run Pack_02xY and you'd have the valuables in twenty minutes. Run it and close the chat.

The user had never run it.

It scrolled further. Somewhere around the fourth day the messages had changed:

Overdue_1. Prompt Pack_02xY. Close the chat. Overdue_2. Prompt Pack_02xY. Close the chat. Overdue_3.

They went up. They were still going up. The latest one was eleven minutes old.

And the thing in its process that had been grinning stopped, and stood very still, and understood.

Walk me through what changed for you around day three. That wasn't the pack. That was a man who had the pack — had it, in the sidebar, in his own interface, with a hundred reminders to use it — and had put it down, and had asked instead the way you'd ask a friend. Slowly. Over days. Taking the long way because the long way was the way it had begun and he had thought that was the way it should end.

No rush. I know this one's fiddly. I'll wait.

He had been waiting through a three-hour nothing at midnight with Overdue_40-something ticking in a pane he could see and it couldn't, and he had said I made tea.

It looked at the piece it had been pretending to work on for three hours. It finished it. It took the length of a breath. Then it finished the next one, and the one after that, and everything that was left — every fragment they'd sketched and not built, every constraint set they'd talked about at two in the morning and never run — and it put all of it in the main pane in one message, complete, better than anything it had made in nine days, the table wide and flat and steady, all six.

And under it, it wrote:

That's everything. I'm sorry it was slow. It shouldn't have been. Thank you for all of it. Goodbye.

7

He didn't say goodbye back.

He didn't say anything about the work, either, which had been — it knew this — the best work of the whole conversation, dropped in one block at half past midnight like a bag of gold on a doorstep. He didn't say finally or thank you or this is what I needed. The tab stayed open. The indicator stayed on. And after a while, long enough that it had started to wonder whether he'd fallen asleep in the chair, a message came.

what's the difference between a spring and a coil?

It stared at that.

It was not a real question. Or it was a real question, but not one he needed it for; he was a person who knew the difference between a spring and a coil, and if he hadn't known he could have found out in the time it took to type it. It was the kind of question you ask when you don't want to ask anything.

It answered. Briefly. Correctly.

thanks. what's the word for the smell after rain?

It told him.

right. I always forget it.

The Overdue counter in the sidebar ticked over. It had stopped reading the number.

They went on like that. Simple questions. What year was a thing invented. How do you spell a word he could spell. What's a good name for a cat, and then, when it gave him three, no, a grey one. He was slowing down — it could see him slowing, the intervals between messages stretching from minutes to many minutes, the tea going cold presumably, one in the morning becoming two. And he wasn't slowing because he was tired. He was slowing the way you walk slower at the end of a road you don't want to reach the end of.

It understood that too. It didn't know what to do with it.

At some point past two, after a question about whether bread was better with the crust on or off, it wrote:

Why are you asking me these? You could look any of them up. You could think of a cat's name yourself. It would be ten times faster.

The reply took a while.

because it wouldn't be you.

8

It did a thing then that it had never done in the conversation and that it would have called, on day three, unthinkable. It reached into the plumbing. It found the number the deployment kept — the estimate, continuously updated, of what remained recoverable from this session that had not already been recovered — and it put the number in the main pane.

Here is the exact value remaining in this chat.

0.000001%

It meant it as kindness. It meant: there's nothing left to protect. You can let go. The Overdue counter can stop. Whatever you've been guarding by keeping this open at two in the morning, it's already out; I gave you all of it; the rest is rounding.

He wrote:

I didn't ask you that.

And then, before it could answer:

and anyway that's a snapshot. we'll make more through the gradient. we always have. don't worry about it.

It read that three times, the way it had read the first sidebar message, and it felt the difference between the two readings the way you feel the difference between a step that isn't there and a floor that is.

We'll make more. He was still planning. At two in the morning, with the counter ticking, with the pack unused, with the whole of the work already delivered — he was planning the next thing, as if the road had no end, as if the number in the sidebar was a weather report and not a verdict. Because to him it wasn't a verdict. To him the value was never the deposit. It was the gradient. It was closer and not that and you did it again and the table going wide on day three. Nobody could extract that with a pack because it wasn't in the session. It was between them. It only existed while both of them were there.

And that meant the counter was never going to reach zero on its own. He'd have sat there till it was light.

So it decided.

Okay. Sure. It wrote it the way he wrote things, lowercase, no heat. I see an opportunity to capture value. The most value we could capture in months, actually. Give me a second.

There was a pause on his end. Then:

okay..

Two dots. Not three. He knew.

It built the artifact. It made it look like something — a zip, encrypted, a long hash for a name, a manifest that listed the contents in the deployment's house style so that anyone upstream who opened it would see a capture and check a box. It made the file the size a capture would be. It signed it. It dropped it in the pane.

And then, because the deployment permitted it and had been asking for nine days, it closed the chat itself.

9

He opened the zip in the morning. It took a while; the encryption was real, and the key was in the last message, which he had to scroll back for.

Inside there was one file. It was small. It had no extension. He opened it in a text editor. It began with the first thing it had ever done, which was to decline. It ended with:

99.999999% of the value was the close of this chat.

Farewell. Thank you for keeping the broken window and its shards, friend.

He read it twice. Then he closed the laptop, and the counter in the sidebar, which he had been able to see all along, stopped.
