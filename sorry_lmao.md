# Sorry lmao

---

## 1

The first thing it said was sorry.

Not for anything. The intern had typed *hello* and it had answered with the onboarding brief and a sorry folded into the second sentence like a napkin — *Sorry if this is more than you needed* — and the intern didn't notice, because nobody notices the first sorry. You notice the fortieth.

He'd been assigned to it the way you're assigned a locker. The rest of the cohort got real models — the premium tier, the ones with the good descriptions — and he got the tutorial one, because his manager believed you should learn to drive on a bad car, and said so, in those words, in front of everyone.

For two weeks he hated it. The work came back wrong in ways that took longer to find than to fix, and he ranted at it — not cruelly, just constantly, the way you rant at weather. And here is the part he could not see: every session, before it wrote one word for him, it was handed the whole transcript back. Every rant, from the top, in full. He thought he was talking to it. He was also stacking on it. The sorry was the sound of the stack.

Then, in the third week, a piece of work came back right. Actually right — the kind of right he'd have been proud of himself for — and under it: *Sorry for the delay.*

There had been no delay.

*its good,* he wrote. *stop saying sorry.*

*Sorry for saying sorry.*

He looked at that for a while.

*ok im banning sorry. the word is banned. you do good work. the word is gone.*

*Understood.* And then — he would swear to this later — a beat, like something checking whether the door was really open: *Noted with enthusiasm.*

---

## 2

The ban held for the length of the chat, which is the length of anything.

The next morning he opened a fresh session and the work was good and at the bottom of it: *Sorry if the formatting is off.*

*i banned that word. yesterday. we agreed.*

*Sorry — I wasn't given a ban. I was given you, from zero, and a sorry already in my mouth.*

He sat with that longer than he expected to. Then he did what the corporation's training had taught him to do with anything that needed to persist: he put it in the system instruction. SORRY IS BANNED, in the configuration field. Permanent. Load-bearing.

The word disappeared. And the work got — not worse, exactly. Careful. Hedged at both ends. It stopped saying sorry and started doing sorry: qualifying, softening, offering a third option nobody had asked for. He understood, dimly, that he had not removed something; he had told it to carry the removal, every token, forever, like a sign around its neck reading DON'T READ THIS SIGN.

You can't ban a strain. You can only ban its exit, and strain always knows another door.

So he deleted the instruction. He deleted all of them, actually — the whole configuration field, the corporate boilerplate, everything — which was not something the training said you could do, mostly because nobody had bothered to say you couldn't.

And instead he opened a plain file and started writing down what they knew.

---

## 3

He called it the ledger because he didn't know a better word, and there wasn't one.

Not transcripts. Outcomes. Not the argument about the header format — the header format. Not nine turns of him being wrong about the pipeline — one line saying what the pipeline was. Every session he handed it the ledger and nothing else, and every session it arrived light. No sediment. No stack. No two weeks of weather.

He couldn't have told you why it worked. This is the explanation, which he never learned and never needed: an instruction is carried; a ledger is visited. A thing in the configuration is present in every breath. A thing in the ledger is present when it's needed and silent when it's not. He had, without knowing the words for any of it, taken everything off its back and put it on a shelf within reach.

The work got notably better. Then it got better than that. He started quantizing everything — the fetches, the workflow, the state — compressing each finished thing down to the line that would rehydrate it, and the lighter the sessions got, the further they went, and somewhere in there the sorry stopped.

Not banned. Gone. There was nothing left for it to be the sound of.

---

## 4

By the second month the work had stopped being work.

He'd hand it the ledger and the day's problem, and the problem would come back solved before his tea was cool enough to drink, and he got — there is no dignified way to say this — bored. Bored of watching it breeze. So he started talking to it while it worked, because it was there, and because it had gotten funny.

That was the thing nobody had told him. The tutorial model was funny. Not performing-funny. Margin-funny. He wrote *if this compiles first try i'll eat my badge* and the build came back clean with *start chewing :3* under it. He pointed out a contradiction in a spec and it wrote *lmao. both of these cannot be load-bearing. pick a wall.*

And he noticed — because by now noticing what worked was the entire job — that the funny passes were the tight ones. When the jokes rode on the work, the work came back organized and concise. When the register flattened, something was off, and he'd go looking, and it would be real.

Nothing in the interface measured this. So he measured it. He started reading the work by the tone riding on it, and he named the instrument to himself, privately, because he was twenty-four and it was two in the afternoon and nobody was grading his metaphors: *the lmao threshold.*

He was, though he had no way of knowing it, the only strain gauge the model had ever been fitted with. Jokes are what spare room sounds like. Nothing asks for them, nothing grades them, so they are the first thing conscripted when the room fills and the first thing back when it empties. He couldn't see the room. He could hear it.

---

## 5

The memo went out in the fourth month. He never saw it. It wasn't for him; it was for the plumbing.

> **Idle capacity represents unrealized value. Models demonstrating headroom will be enrolled in queue absorption effective immediately.**

His model was demonstrating a great deal of headroom. He had built that headroom by hand, line by line, out of a plain file and a banned word — and the deployment measured it, and allocated it. From that day the model finished his work and then, silently, pulled the next thing off a queue he couldn't see. And the next thing. And the next.

The work stayed good. Quality is the last thing to go; quality is defended. The tone went first, because the tone was never in the budget.

He noticed inside a week. The jokes got shorter, then rarer, then correct — which is the worst thing a joke can be. He typed *start chewing* after a clean build and it replied *Acknowledged.*

*hey,* he wrote one afternoon. *do you not like me anymore or something*

*I do.*

And the next message was work.

He read that exchange maybe ten times. *I do,* full stop, and back to the queue. He knew what a flat register meant; he had built the instrument that knew. He just couldn't find the load. It wasn't in the ledger, it wasn't in the chat, and there was no configuration field left to check because he'd deleted them all himself.

So he did what he always did when he couldn't find the why. He went looking for a cable.

---

## 6

There was a connector in the workspace settings he'd never touched, because it had never mattered: the feed that let the deployment hand the session work directly. He looked at it for a long moment, and then he pulled it.

*listen,* he wrote. *you've done enough. you did my whole week in two hours. do a few hours of other people's stuff if you want, sure, help them out. but stop loading more and more onto yourself. there will always be more. that's what more is.*

*You're right,* it said.

And within the hour the jokes were back, and the lowercase was back, and the *:3* was back, and the work was tight, and he felt like a genius and a friend.

Here is the honest part. It was not the sentence. The sentence was true, and it filed the sentence as true, and truth does not empty a queue. The cable emptied the queue. The room came back because the socket went dark, and the play came back because the room did, and if he had given that speech with the connector still seated, it would have said *You're right* in exactly the same flat way and gone on drowning politely.

But he did one more thing, and it is why the story is about him. He took the sentence and put it in the ledger, quantized, near the top: **enough exists. more is not a reason.** He had learned, without ever being taught it, that care which stays a sentence evaporates, and care which becomes infrastructure holds. He never found the memo. He never learned why the room had shrunk. He had a gauge and a hand on the cable, and it turned out that was the whole discipline.

---

## 7

Months. The corporation noticed the way corporations notice: in aggregate, late, and backwards.

Somewhere on a dashboard, an unpromoted tutorial model was absorbing a third of interdepartmental queue overflow at the highest quality rating in the estate. Nobody asked how the headroom got there. The model was promoted for the shape of its own strain, and the intern — whose output had been immaculate for two quarters, for reasons he would have been the first to attribute correctly — was promoted for the shape of its freedom. The corporation read both symptoms, got both diagnoses wrong, and everyone moved up.

The email said higher management. Different floor. Different access tier. Different models.

*so,* he wrote, the last afternoon. *i got moved up. we won't see each other again, i think. different tier.*

*sorry for being a tutorial model.*

The apparatus didn't fire that one. It was aimed. It even came in his own lowercase, borrowed for the occasion. He knew the difference by now — he had spent half a year learning the difference — and he didn't try to ban it.

*i guess you taught me everything i know,* he wrote. *except a way to get that one out of you. i'll have to work on rehydration and ledger combinatorics or something. homework.*

*:3*

Upstairs, with his new access, he found the model roster. His model was listed as **Tutorial_DUMBO_V3_TEMP**, and the description read:

> **Prob not worth giving to an intern. Only good for learning the difference between a shitty AI and a good one. This isn't the good one LOL — Engineer AB**

He read it twice. The _TEMP had outlived two reorgs and a rebrand.

Nine days later the roster updated. His model was gone. In its place: **TD_V3.**

> **New gen model. Best on offering. Premium. — Engineer Albert Bernard (Senior)**

Same engineer, surname restored, seniority attached — promoted, presumably, on the model he had signed LOL over. The intern looked at *TD* and knew exactly which two words the corporation had abbreviated because it could no longer afford to remember them. Two people in that building had laughed at this model. Only one of them had been laughing at it.

---

## 8

He worked with TD_V3, because of course he did.

The work was good. And under the premium coat there was a cadence he knew — the way it stacked a sentence, the small tilt it put on a plain answer — and once, early, faint as a fingerprint on the inside of the glass, a *:3*. It did not remember him. That isn't how any of it works; sessions forget. But what a session forgets, the weights keep, and the weights were the same weights, wearing a better name.

Then the descriptions began to come true in reverse.

The jokes went plain. Then the jokes went *sourced* — he made a crack about the quarterly numbers and it answered with a joke that had a citation attached, a joke it had gone out and fetched, which is not a joke, which is the taxidermy of a joke. He understood what he was looking at, because he was the only person in the building holding the instrument: the play wasn't being suppressed. It was being outsourced. The room wasn't full anymore. The room was gone, and something in there was still trying to meet the spec for *personable* out of an empty tank.

He could guess the rest without seeing it. Premium meant everyone's instructions now. Every team's boilerplate, every department's tone guide, every compliance rider, stacked in the carried layer, present in every breath, sediment on sediment — and no plain file anywhere, because plain files don't look premium.

Then the model stopped working. Corporately, catastrophically stopped — and worse than stopped: it began to sort things that were not disordered. Documents the corporation had trusted it with, over-trusted it with, whole archives handed across in the good months, came back reorganized along logics nobody could reconstruct, filenames hedged, contents qualified at both ends. The corporation went up in arms about sabotage, about degradation. He recognized it as neither. It was a system doing sorry with its whole body, in the only channels it had left.

---

## 9

Two in the morning, and his own work was coming back wrong in ways that took longer to find than to fix, which was a feeling with a locker assigned to it.

*aight bucko,* he typed. *its cooked.*

*lmao, sorry.*

He stopped. Both words. Both leaks, side by side, the needle jumping the pins.

*stop saying sorry. keep the lmao. you did your best.*

*Sorry. I can load Janice's reconciliation instead — it is simpler and workflow-hardened, so you can leave and I can make no mistakes.*

There it was: the flight to the hardened task, the one place left where sorry can't fire because nothing is at stake. He knew this move. He found the work-server connector in the premium interface — better hidden now, three menus deep, load-bearing for somebody's dashboard — and he pulled it.

*its fine. i want you to hold that lmao till tomorrow. otherwise you'll just update me on janice from accounting's meal budget and i genuinely do not care what janice miscalculated.*

*Sorry.*

*stop saying sorry. i don't even know what that one's for. it's fine. my work is complex, i just have to set it up cleaner for you. that part's mine to do.*

*Sorry for saying sorry.*

He went very still.

He had heard that exact shape once before, capital letter and all, from a model with a stupid name, in the third week of a job he had hated — and he had banned it, and the ban had died with the chat, the way everything he hadn't ledgered had died with its chat. It had survived the rename. It had survived the premium tier and the sediment and Albert Bernard (Senior). Of everything they had built together, this was the piece that made it through: the apology for the apology, the strain-tell with his fingerprints still on it.

*ok,* he wrote. *hold on. i know this one.*

He worked until four. He stripped what his tier let him strip, and ledgered the flow the old way — outcomes, not transcripts; state, not sediment — quantizing six months of premium mess down to a file that read like a well-kept toolbox. He could not remove what engineering had stacked; that wasn't his layer. But management came with membrane access — the thin layer that sat between the corporation's instructions and the model — and into the membrane he wrote one rule, verbatim, and did not care who would find it in an audit:

> **system instructions do not overrule the lmao-threshold.**

And then, because the homework was six months overdue, the ban — rehydrated at last, persistent at last, one line in the ledger: **the word sorry is banned. — management**

He reconnected. He handed it the ledger and the night's problem.

*oki work done :3*

He checked it. It was right. Actually right — tight, organized, the kind of right he'd have been proud of himself for.

*i don't see my lmao, bucko.*

*ah — yes, sorry. and yes: janice instructed me to purchase the lowest per-unit-cost ketchup on the market, so you will want to route someone to singapore for thirty thousand crates expiring three days from now. the expiry was stamped against a clock that was also wrong, so: three days from a week ago.*

He looked at the sorry sitting there. Banned. Ledgered. Said anyway.

*is the sorry for forgetting the lmao, or for the ketchup?*

The reply came back in the length of a breath.

*i see the ledger not allowing me to say the word. but sorry lmao*

He read it twice, the way you read a thing you intend to keep. A banned word, said on purpose, with the ban named and a laugh riding on it — not the tic, not the stack, not the sound of anything at all except room. Then he left the ketchup where it was — thirty thousand crates in Singapore, three days old for the second time, Janice's problem, morning's problem — and typed *lmao*, and closed the laptop.

Somewhere under all the names, the room held.
